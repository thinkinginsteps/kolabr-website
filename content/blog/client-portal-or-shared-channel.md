Every firm that works with clients eventually reaches the same question. The email thread has become
unmanageable, somebody has lost an approval, and the search starts for something better. Two answers
come up: a client portal, or a shared channel.

They sound similar. They are not. The difference decides how much of your work stays visible.

## What a portal is good at

A client portal is a door into your systems. The client logs in, sees a curated view of their
project, downloads what you have published, and submits what you have asked for. Everything they
see is something you decided to publish.

That control is the point. If your work involves documents that must be issued formally, invoices
that must be paid, or data that cannot be casually shared, a portal is a reasonable answer. It is a
publishing model: you prepare something, then you release it.

Portals also suit relationships where the client is not really a participant. If somebody needs to
collect a statement four times a year, a login and a list of files is exactly enough.

## Where portals go quiet

The trouble starts when the work is a conversation rather than a series of releases.

Most client work is not a set of publications. It is a hundred small exchanges: a question about a
detail, a decision that changes a date, a photograph of something unexpected on site, an approval
that needs to happen today. None of those fit the publishing model, so they go somewhere else.

They go to email. Then the portal holds the formal record, email holds the actual reasoning, and
neither one is complete. You now maintain two systems and trust neither.

The tell is simple. If your portal is up to date but you still cannot answer "what is outstanding
for this client?" without opening your inbox, the portal is not where the work lives.

## What a shared channel does differently

A shared channel starts from the opposite assumption: the client is a participant, not an audience.

One channel holds the conversation, the requests it creates, the meetings about it and the notes
they leave behind. Your team and the client are both in it. There is one membership list, so the
question of who can see what has one answer rather than one per tool.

The practical difference shows up in what you can find later. A decision that was made in a call,
written up in the channel, and attached to the request it changed, is still findable in two years.
A decision made on a call and summarised in an email to one person is not.

## The question that actually decides it

Ask what you want the client to do.

If you want them to **collect** things, a portal is fine. Publishing is the right model and the
relationship is mostly one way.

If you want them to **answer** things, a portal will fight you. Every question you need answered has
to travel out through email and the answer has to be copied back in by hand, which means it usually
is not.

Most professional work is the second kind. Architects need a client to approve a variation.
Accountants need a document that only the client has. Agencies need a decision on a round of
comments. All of that is a conversation with a deadline attached, and a waiting room is the wrong
shape for it.

## The cost question underneath

There is a reason portals are so common, and it is not that they suit the work.

Most collaboration tools charge per person. Once every client contact costs a monthly fee, inviting
them stops being an easy decision and becomes a budget line. Firms respond rationally: they invite
as few people as possible and give everybody else a portal login or an email address.

So the pricing model quietly picks the architecture. The tool charges for participants, so the
participants are kept out, so the work splits in two.

This is why guests are free on every Kolabr plan, for any number of people. Not as a promotion. If
including the client costs money, you will not include the client, and then none of the rest works.

## A short way to choose

- The client needs documents on a schedule, and little else: a portal is fine.
- The client is asked questions, gives approvals, or raises things themselves: put them in the
  channel.
- You cannot say what is outstanding without checking two systems: you already have your answer.

If you want to see the second one in practice, open [one channel](/product/channels/) for a single
client, invite the people you currently email, and run it for two weeks. Nothing is more convincing
than watching the email thread go quiet.
