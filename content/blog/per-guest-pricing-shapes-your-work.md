Pricing pages look like commercial documents. They are also design documents, because the thing a
tool charges for is the thing you will use less of.

Most collaboration tools charge per person per month. That is a reasonable way to sell software to a
company, where everyone is on one payroll and the headcount is known. It becomes strange the moment
the work involves people outside the company, which for most firms is where the work actually is.

## The decision that gets made for you

Say a client relationship involves five people on their side: a main contact, someone who approves
money, and three others who hold information you need.

At a typical per-user price, inviting all five costs somewhere between a few hundred and a few
thousand a year, per client. Multiply by forty clients and it is a budget line somebody has to
defend.

So nobody invites five. They invite one, and the other four get email. Perfectly rational, and it
quietly sets the architecture of the whole operation:

- The conversation splits between the tool and email, so neither is complete.
- The four people who were not invited answer questions in threads nobody else can see.
- The one person who was invited becomes a relay, copying things across by hand.
- When that person is away, the work stops.

Nobody decided this. The pricing model decided it.

## The workarounds are worse than they look

Firms are inventive, and the usual workarounds each carry a cost.

**A shared login.** Cheap, and it destroys the record. Every action is attributed to
"clientaccess@", which is exactly the attribution you need when something is disputed later.

**A view-only guest.** Cheaper than a full seat in some tools, and it turns a participant into an
audience. They can read, but they cannot answer, so the answers still arrive by email.

**One channel for everything.** Invite the client to a single shared space and keep the real
conversation elsewhere. Now you have two conversations and a client who suspects it.

**Just use email.** The honest one, and still the most common. It works right up until somebody
leaves, or a decision is questioned, or you need to know what is outstanding.

## What it costs to get this wrong

The cost is not the licence fee. It is the second system.

When people who matter cannot be in the tool, email becomes the real place the work happens, and
everything valuable ends up there: the reasoning behind a change, the approval, the promise about a
date. Email is private to its recipients, unstructured, and gone when somebody's mailbox is closed.

So the firm pays for a collaboration tool and keeps its institutional memory in the one place it
cannot search, count or hand over.

## The alternative

Charge for the people whose work the software organises, and let everyone else in for nothing.

That is how Kolabr is priced. You pay for your own team. Clients, contractors, parents, trustees,
funders and suppliers join as guests, free, on every plan, for any number of people. A guest sees
only the channels they were added to, and nothing about your other clients.

It is not generosity, it is the only way the argument holds together. A product that says "put the
work and the people in one place" and then charges you per person is arguing with itself.

## What to ask a vendor

If you are comparing tools, three questions cut through most pricing pages:

1. **What does a client contact cost, per month, in practice?** Not the guest tier's name. The
   number on the invoice when a client has five people.
2. **What can a guest actually do?** Read only, or take part? Can they be in more than one space?
3. **What happens to the record when they leave?** Does the history stay with the work, with names
   and dates attached?
