Ask anyone in a client-facing team what is outstanding right now and watch what happens. They open
their inbox. They scroll. They say "let me check with Sarah". The honest answer is that nobody
knows, because the list only exists in several people's heads and one very long thread.

This is not a discipline problem. It is a format problem. Requests arrive as messages, and a message
has no owner, no due date and no state. It is just text that somebody hopefully remembers.

## Why the inbox fails at this

An inbox is optimised for one person receiving things, not for a team tracking them.

**It is private by default.** A request sent to one person is invisible to everyone else. When that
person is on leave, the request effectively does not exist.

**It has no state.** An email is read or unread. That tells you nothing about whether the work is
done, blocked, waiting on the client, or forgotten.

**It cannot be counted.** You cannot answer "how many things are we late on?" by looking at an
inbox, which means nobody asks, which means the answer stays unknown until a client asks it for you.

**It loses the thread.** The original ask, the clarification, the decision and the delivery end up in
four separate threads, usually with different people copied in.

## What a tracked request needs

Four things, and only four. Any more and people stop doing it.

1. **An owner.** One named person, not a team address. Shared responsibility is nobody's
   responsibility.
2. **A category.** So similar work can be counted, routed and improved. Keep the list short enough
   to remember.
3. **A date or a response target.** Not necessarily a deadline for finishing, but a promise about
   when somebody will respond, which is what clients actually care about.
4. **A state everyone can see.** Including the client, so nobody has to ask for a status update.

Notice what is not on the list: estimates, story points, dependencies, a workflow with nine stages.
All of that is for a project tool. Client requests need less than you think.

## Put it where the conversation already is

The usual failure of request tracking is that it lives somewhere else. The client talks in email, the
team tracks in a separate system, and somebody has to copy between them. That person stops copying
during a busy week, and the system quietly dies.

The fix is not more discipline. It is removing the copying.

In Kolabr, a [request](/product/requests/) is created from the message that started it. The client
writes in the channel as they always would, and one step turns that message into a tracked item with
an owner, a category, a priority and a response target attached. The conversation and the record are
the same object, so there is nothing to keep in sync.

Because the client is in the channel as a free guest, they can see the state themselves. Most
"any update?" emails exist only because the answer was not visible anywhere.

## Categories that earn their place

Categories are the part people overthink. A useful set has these properties:

- Somebody could sort a week of requests into them without hesitating.
- Each one has a different response expectation. If two categories have the same target and the same
  owner, they are one category.
- They match how the client thinks, not your internal structure.

For a property manager that might be maintenance, arrears, approvals and notices. For an IT
provider, incidents, changes, access and questions. Start with four, and add one only when you keep
having to think about where something goes.

## Response targets, honestly set

A response target is a promise. The point is not to look impressive; it is to be believed.

Set them from what you already achieve on a normal week, not from what you wish you achieved. A
promise of four hours that you miss twice a week is worse than a promise of one working day that you
always keep, because the first one teaches clients to chase and the second teaches them to wait.

When a target is going to be missed, the client should find out from the system rather than from
silence. That single change removes most of the chasing from a client relationship.

## What you get on the other side

Once requests have an owner and a clock, three questions become answerable in seconds: what is
outstanding, who has too much, and where the same request keeps coming from. The third is the
valuable one. Most recurring requests are a missing page in the [wiki](/product/wiki/) or a broken
process, and you cannot see the pattern until the work is countable.

None of this requires a big rollout. Pick one client, agree four categories, and run everything
through the channel for a fortnight. At the end of it, you will be able to answer the question that
started this article without opening your inbox.
