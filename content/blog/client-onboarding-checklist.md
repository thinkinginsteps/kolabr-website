Onboarding usually gets treated as paperwork: a contract, an invoice, a kick-off call, then straight
into the work. Six months later somebody asks who approved the change to the scope, and the answer
is in a call nobody wrote up.

The first week is cheap to get right and expensive to redo. Here is a checklist that takes about an
hour per client.

## 1. Decide who is actually in the room

Not the people on the contract. The people who will be asked questions.

Write the list out: the client's day to day contact, whoever signs off money, whoever holds the
information you will need, and any contractor or supplier whose work touches yours. For a building
that is the trustee, the managing agent and three contractors. For an audit it is the finance lead,
the bookkeeper and often the client's own client.

If your tool charges per person, you are about to ration this list. That is worth noticing, because
the people you leave out are exactly the ones who will slow the work down later. Guests are free on
every Kolabr plan for this reason.

## 2. Create one place, not four

One [channel](/product/channels/) per client, project or matter, holding the conversation, the
requests, the meetings and the wiki. One membership list decides who sees all of it.

The test of whether you have got this right: a new person joins in month four and can answer "what
is going on with this client?" by reading, without asking anyone.

## 3. Agree the categories before the work starts

Four is usually enough. Maintenance, arrears, approvals, notices. Or incidents, changes, access,
questions. Pick them in the first week, while the relationship is still abstract, because arguing
about categories during a busy month never happens.

For each one, agree what a reasonable response looks like and write it down where the client can see
it. Response targets you actually hit build more trust than ambitious ones you miss.

## 4. Write the three pages that always get asked for

Every client relationship has a handful of questions that come up again and again. Write them once,
in the channel [wiki](/product/wiki/), in the first week:

- **How we work together.** Who does what, how to raise something, what happens when it is urgent.
- **The facts about this client.** Site addresses, system names, account numbers, access details,
  the things that live in one person's head today.
- **The decisions log.** Started empty, added to every time something is agreed that costs money,
  time or liability.

The third page is the one that saves you. A year later, "we never agreed that" is answered with a
date and a name rather than a search through somebody's sent items.

## 5. Put the recurring dates in, once

Monthly review, quarterly report, annual renewal, the inspection that must happen before the
certificate expires. Set them up as [scheduled events](/product/meetings/) in the channel now, while
you are thinking about them, rather than trusting somebody to remember in eight months.

## 6. Say out loud how this will work

The most skipped step. Tell the client, in one short message, what you have set up and what you
expect from them: raise things here, we will answer in this time, here is where to find the history,
here is who owns what.

People fall back on email when they are unsure what the alternative is. Ten lines at the start
prevents most of that.

## 7. Book the review that checks it worked

Two weeks in, look at what actually happened. Did anything important arrive by email anyway? Did a
category turn out to be wrong? Is somebody missing who should have been invited?

Two weeks is early enough to fix, and late enough to know.

## The checklist, short version

1. List the people who will be asked questions, including outside the client's own team.
2. One channel per client, one membership list.
3. Four categories, with honest response targets.
4. Three wiki pages: how we work, the facts, the decisions log.
5. Recurring dates in the calendar now.
6. A short message telling the client how this works.
7. A review booked for two weeks in.
