Kolabr vs Basecamp

Basecamp organises work into projects. Kolabr organises it around the people you work with, in a channel that does not end when one job does. Both let you bring clients in for free, so the real comparison is what happens to the relationship after the project closes, and whether anyone can tell what was promised while it was open.

Jump to the comparison See pricing

The short version

A project ends. A client does not. Kolabr gives each relationship one channel that holds every request, every document and every conversation you have ever had with them, instead of an archive of finished projects you have to search through.

Proof takes one week

Take your longest-standing client, the one whose work never really stops, and give them a channel. By Friday you will have the whole relationship in one place, with every open item owned and dated.

Most work does not have an end date

A project-shaped tool works beautifully for a thing with a beginning and an end: a launch, a build, a move. It starts to strain when the work is a relationship. A client you have had for six years is not a project. Neither is support, a retainer, a maintenance contract or a site you look after. Those produce a new project every few months and split the history across all of them.

A Kolabr channel is the relationship itself. The client joins once and stays. Work arrives inside it as requests, closes, and is still findable in two years alongside the wiki article that explains how you handled it. Nobody is re-invited, and nothing is archived away from the conversation that produced it.

Tasks live inside the request, and die with it

Kolabr has tasks. What it does not have is a task manager. Every task belongs to the request that created it, is assigned to somebody, and disappears when that request is closed. There is no separate list to groom, no board that slowly fills with items nobody remembers agreeing to, and no second place to look for what is outstanding.

This is a deliberate limit. Free-standing to-do lists are easy to create and almost impossible to keep honest, and after a year they describe a version of the work that stopped being true months ago. Tying every task to a request means the list is always as current as the request it belongs to, and closing the request closes the loop.

One home. What is outstanding for a client is the list of open requests. There is no second answer.
No orphans. A task cannot exist without a reason for existing, because it cannot exist without a request.
Nothing to tidy. Close the request and its tasks go with it, so there is no backlog grooming ritual.

A to-do is not a promise with a clock on it

The gap between assigning work internally and answering to a client is where most tools stop. Kolabr closes it.

Requests have a category

An approval, a variation, an incident and a question are different things with different urgency, and they route to different people.

Requests have a clock

A response-time target per category, visible to the client, so “we are looking at it” becomes a date they can hold you to.

Requests come from conversation

The client describes the problem in the channel and one step turns it into a numbered request with the thread attached. Nothing is retyped.

Requests roll up

One view shows what is open, overdue or unassigned across every client at once, instead of opening each project in turn.

What Kolabr does not try to be

Worth knowing before you move, so nothing surprises you in month three.

A planning tool

No Gantt charts, dependency chains or resource levelling. If you plan a build week by week, keep the tool that does it and run the client side in Kolabr.

A standalone task manager

Tasks exist inside requests and nowhere else. That is the design, not a gap waiting to be filled.

A time-tracking system

Requests tell you what was asked and when it was answered. They do not replace whatever you bill from.

A one-day migration

Start with one client channel and let the rest follow. Nothing has to be exported before you can see whether it works.

What each one actually costs you

Basecamp Plus is $15 per user per month billed monthly, and Pro Unlimited is a flat $299 per month billed annually, or $349 monthly, for any number of people. Kolabr is $9.99, $19.99 or $39.99 per user per month. Neither product charges for clients or contractors.

Above about twenty staff the flat plan is the lower number, so compare what the number buys. What your plan buys is seats per channel: 30 on Starter, 100 on Pro, unlimited on Max, shared between your people and your guests.

Questions people ask before moving

Is Kolabr a project management tool?

No, and that is deliberate. Kolabr organises work by relationship rather than by project: a channel per client, site or department, which carries on after any single job ends. Inside it, work is tracked as requests with owners and deadlines. If you need Gantt charts and resource levelling, use a planning tool for that and run the client conversation in Kolabr.

Does Kolabr have tasks?

Yes, inside a request. A request can carry its own checklist of tasks, assigned and ticked off by the people working on it, and those tasks close when the request closes. What Kolabr deliberately does not offer is a free-standing task manager, because lists that float loose from the thing that caused them are how a tool fills up with work nobody remembers agreeing to.

Both offer free guests, so what is the difference?

Structure. In Basecamp a client is invited to a project and re-invited to the next one. In Kolabr a client has a channel that holds every piece of work you do together, so the history, the wiki and the open requests stay in one place across years rather than being split across archived projects.

What about ongoing work that never really ends?

This is the common reason firms outgrow a project-shaped tool. Support, retainers, maintenance and standing client relationships have no finish date, so they sit uneasily in something built around projects. A Kolabr channel has no end date, and requests come and go inside it.

Which is cheaper?

At a flat $299 per month, Basecamp stops charging per person after a certain size, so for a large team it is the lower number. Below roughly twenty people, per-seat pricing applies on both sides and they are closer. The more useful question is what each price includes: Kolabr covers requests with SLAs, a wiki, video calls and scheduled events without another subscription.

Can we move from Basecamp to Kolabr?

Yes, and the usual approach is to stop creating new projects rather than migrate old ones. Open a channel for your busiest client, run the next piece of work there, and let the Basecamp archive stay readable for as long as you need it.
